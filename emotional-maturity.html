Philosophy capstone paper

Emotional Maturity Is a Skill

Introduction

Our natural tendency to evaluate one another’s social and emotional lives often manifests in the usage of the words “mature” and “immature”. We praise actors that act “maturely” in emotionally difficult situations, and criticize others for acting “immaturely”, and learn to both cultivate a degree of this “maturity” in each other, as a parent might do for their child, and expect it, as a person might expect from their significant other. Despite this ubiquity in the usage of these terms in daily life, it seems that philosophers of emotion have devoted little attention to exploring its meaning and theorizing about it. Extensive philosophical work examines emotion rationality (Ach’s and Na’aman 2007; D’Arms and Jacobson 2000; de Sousa 1979) and emotion regulation (Fischer and Manstead 2016; Uusberg et. al. 2017); however, they do not adequately capture the concept of emotional maturity. This is surprising, especially considering the large body of research that discusses the role of emotions as serving crucial social functions (Fischer and Manstead 2016), and how communities develop norms dictating appropriateness of emotional attitudes in various contexts (Van Wietmarschen 2021, Creasy 2022). This paper aims to bridge that gap by offering an account of emotional maturity that describes it as a fundamentally interpersonal, situation-relative skill with two essential components. One, is the ability to gauge the distribution of emotional labor among involved actors in a given context, and the other is the ability to respond to this assessment in order to either maintain an equitable distribution, or rebalance load to achieve one. I distinguish emotional maturity from common terms within the literature such as emotional rationality and emotional intelligence, and then present my positive account, defining its key components. Finally, I demonstrate its applicability through case studies and explore how everyday discourse pertaining to “emotional maturity” builds or hinders interpersonal relationships.

Emotional Maturity

Existing literature in the philosophy of emotion focuses extensively on emotional rationality. Traditional assessments of rationality typically assess emotions against three dimensions: fit, warrant, and benefit (Kerr 2019; D’Arms and Jacobson 2000). To summarize, an agent’s emotion is fitting if and only if the emotion corresponds to relevant features and the object of the emotion. An agent’s emotion is warranted if and only if the agent has sufficient evidence for the fittingness of the emotion in that situation. Finally, an agent’s emotion is beneficial if and only if it contributes to the well-being of the agent.

Assessing emotions along these dimensions provides structure to the internal deliberation involved in emotional decision-making leading to expression and regulation. Fischer and Manstead (2016) talk about how this regulation serves a crucial social function through affiliation or distancing, placing this in a more interpersonal lens. This work of managing, expressing, and regulating emotions is termed “emotional labor” (Hochschild 1983). Research demonstrates that emotional labor is often distributed inequitably, and expectations around who should shoulder this burden is heavily influenced by gender, race, class, and profession, leading to certain communities being exploited for their care (McKittrick-Sweitzer 2023).

Consider the following case: Jim and Della are romantic partners. During their arguments, Della often becomes upset and tearful due to Jim’s words, assigning blame, and Jim feels guilty. Jim’s guilt is fitting because his words did hurt her feelings. It is warranted because Della’s tears offer adequate evidence that he has caused her pain. Jim then regulates his emotions in order to suppress his frustrations and de-escalate the conflict. His reaction is also beneficial because it maintains the harmony in their relationship. Yet, this analysis seems lacking. Jim fails to recognize that he is performing disproportionate emotional labor in the relationship, and is now undertaking the task of managing both his emotions and Della’s. He might think that he is being a good significant other rather than recognizing that emotional labor should be equitably distributed among involved parties.

In this case, Jim has acted rationally because his emotion is fitting, warranted, and beneficial. However, I argue that his response in this situation lacks emotional maturity. He is unable to recognize the system of emotional labor distribution within which he operates, and therefore is unable to work towards achieving a more equitable arrangement. This suggests that emotional maturity and rationality are not the same. It requires a degree of awareness of distribution of emotional labor and responsive action towards making that distribution more equitable.

I propose that an agent displays emotional maturity in a given situation if and only if they (1) recognize how emotional labor is distributed among involved actors, including awareness of inequities, and (2) respond in ways that aim to create or preserve equitable distributions with consideration for how an action (or strategic inaction) may affect relevant parties.

This account of emotional maturity hinges on it being understood not as an individual character trait, but as an instance-specific and situation-relative skill that can be taught and learned. The first requirement is that the agent is aware of the existing distribution of emotional labor. By emotional labor, I am referring to the concept introduced by Hochschild (1983) in her book The Managed Heart, regarding the process by which people are “expected to manage their feelings in accordance with organizationally defined rules and guidelines” (Wharton 2009). This may include acts such as conflict resolution, managing another person’s emotions, providing care and emotional support, suppressing one’s emotions, and regulation, among others. Recognizing emotional labor distribution simply means recognizing such patterns and understanding not only what another person might be feeling, but also what kind of emotional labor they may be performing, who else may be performing such work, and an assessment of whether or not each party is responsible for an equitable share.

Recognition alone, however, is insufficient. The second requirement is that this awareness leads to some deliberation that eventually results in an action taken to either maintain the status quo or redistribute the burden more equitably, with consideration for the impact an action might take on involved parties. This could look like setting a boundary, refusing to perform labor that is inequitably assigned, or asking under-burdened parties to contribute their share. In a case where emotional labor is already equitably assigned, or where any action might result in steep escalation or harm to an involved party, then the agent may strategically choose a path of inaction to display emotional maturity. This requires empathy, social context awareness, and practice in reading these patterns.

It is important to emphasize that by this account, emotional maturity is a buildable skill. This means that just as communities shape appropriate emotional expression through social norms (Van Wietmarschen 2021; Creasy 2022), emotional maturity is a skill that can be obtained through experience and reflection, and is inherently formed by community.

Illustrative Cases

Rational, not mature

Consider Malcolm, the eldest of four siblings in a family. Malcolm’s response to emotional conflict within the family is anxiety, and he often assumes the responsibility of mediator among his siblings. His anxiety is fitting because there is interpersonal tension in close quarters, warranted because there is evidence of unresolved issues in the environment, and beneficial because his role as a mediator maintains harmony within the family and peace between members. However, Malcolm does not recognize the fact that he has been charged with the duty of “emotion manager” in the family. He thinks he is being a good son and sibling rather than recognizing that such emotional labor should be distributed proportionate to existing conflict resolution skills within a family.

Irrational, but mature

Consider the case of public school teachers presented by McKittrick-Sweitzer (2023) while introducing the concept of care exploitation. Public school teachers are expected to be dedicated to providing education “even with abysmal pay and little appreciation”. Say there is a teacher Sara who has worked extremely hard for many years, helping students within and outside the classroom to develop as well-rounded individuals. When a legislative body announces that there would be another round of budget cuts and no raises for the coming cycle, Sara feels angry and decides to stop working beyond contract hours. This response is fitting because the exploitation is genuine, warranted because there is clear evidence of systematic mistreatment, but perhaps unbeneficial because it would hurt students and her reputation as a teacher. Her response may be considered irrational, but it is mature because she is able to recognize that the school system is exploitative of her caring disposition and expects her to bear a disproportionate amount of emotional labor because she cares about her students. By refusing to enable this systematic exploitation and setting a boundary despite potential negative consequences in the immediate future, Sara shows emotional maturity.

Both rational and mature

Recall the example about Jim and Della presented in the previous section. In this case, instead of suppressing his feelings and shouldering all the responsibility of de-escalation, Jim recognizes the pattern and addresses it directly, apologizing for hurting Della while also naming the pattern that he has observed and proposing that they discuss how both of them might contribute more equitably to this de-escalation. Jim’s response remains rational, while also demonstrating that he is able to recognize the skew in the distribution of emotional labor and proposing a more equitable arrangement without being inflammatory or inconsiderate of Della’s emotional state. He is both rational and mature in this scenario.

Objections

Individual agency in a community-centric account

One may worry that defining emotional maturity as situation-relative and shaped by communal norms about emotional labor distribution would lead to it ceasing to have individual meaning. This argument may propose that framing emotional maturity as being mainly about social patterns risks erasing personal responsibility (A is a product of A’s community) or reducing maturity to compliance with group norms (A’s community says labor distribution X is “mature”, so it must be true).

My account does not erase individual agency, but rather finds it in the capacity to recognize and respond to communal patterns critically. Being emotionally mature requires one to become cognizant of the systems of emotional labor distributions within which they operate, and how best to participate in or resist those systems. This does not equate to complying with the norms; I argue it is an individual’s personal responsibility to exercise their judgement to critique and refute or support an existing norm, as well as developing this skill over time.

General distributive justice

Another concern may be that this account of emotional maturity simply redescribes the problem of distributive justice in a specific subclass of cases.

Emotional maturity is specifically about recognizing and responding to distributions of emotional labor, which requires emotional self-awareness and empathy. The key here is that it specifically concerns emotional labor, which is a skill that requires a level of emotional understanding and strategic navigation that concerns of fairness simply do not process. Thus, emotional maturity is distinctively emotional in both content and method, despite involving distributive concerns.

Conclusion

This paper has offered a fresh account of emotional maturity as an instance-relative skill with two integral components: recognizing the emotional labor distribution and responding to create or preserve equitable distributions. By distinguishing between emotional maturity and emotional rationality, I have attempted to demonstrate the existing gap in philosophy of emotion discourse and defend why this should be considered a novel idea. Understanding emotional maturity as an attribute of a person in a situation rather than an individual trait, and treating it as fundamentally about responding to the observed distribution of emotional labor offers a framework for building healthier interpersonal relationships and carefully examining how emotions in social structures interplay with power dynamics and inequality.

Future Directions

I think this account has both theoretical and practical significance. It fills a gap in the literature by analyzing a concept that is incredibly central to everyday evaluation yet philosophically undertheorized. Future work might explore how this account applies to collective emotional maturity and how groups adapt to their own patterns of emotional labor distribution. There is also an interesting conversation to be had about using the term “emotionally mature” as an exploitative, corrective, or protective call to action in another person to react in a certain way. This investigation may lead to a better understanding of how these dynamics lead to power structures in society, and illuminate how we as individuals may develop an awareness of when emotional responses serve justice, and when they may be detrimental.
